📈Case study

NYC agencies → WeightWatchers · 2015–2020

From QTP and Java Selenium to Protractor, Appium and native

Legacy QTP scripts moved to Java Selenium, a Protractor framework built from scratch, then 200+ Appium tests rebuilt in native Espresso plus hundreds more in XCUITest. Flakiness became rare, and the first demo ran so fast we replayed it in slow motion.

Web: QTP to Selenium and Protractor. At the NYC Department of Health I converted legacy QTP scripts to Selenium WebDriver in Java. At the Department of Sanitation I moved all of the department's QTP/UFT scripts to Selenium WebDriver, ending its HP license costs, then built a Protractor framework from scratch for an AngularJS app. Coming from Java, I had to learn JavaScript and TypeScript and survive JavaScript's infamous promise hell: .then().then().then().then(), until somewhere around the fourth one you stop, scratch your head, and wonder where you even are. I made it out the other side and trained a small team to use the framework.

Mobile: the problem. At WeightWatchers, our Appium suite was slow and unreliable. Tests leaned on brittle XPath locators and hard-coded sleeps, sessions often timed out or hung mid-run, and the drivers lagged behind new Android and iOS releases. A full run took about five times longer than running the same tests by hand. We were working for the framework instead of it working for us, and hundreds of tests sat idle and went stale.

The approach. We moved to the platforms' own test frameworks, Espresso from Google and XCUITest from Apple, dropping Appium's extra server layer. Both were new, with little documentation or community help, so another QA engineer and I worked it out ourselves, Android first. Espresso runs inside the app, so we replaced sleeps with its built-in synchronization and IdlingResources: checks run only when the app is idle. We wrote the tests in Java, then moved them to Kotlin. Even the tooling fought us: early Android Studio builds crashed at times, and a clean build could take several tries and a cleared Gradle cache.

iOS and CI. The iOS app shared the same design, so we mirrored the plan with XCUITest. I learned Swift and used accessibility identifiers for stable locators. Both suites ran on Jenkins for pull-request checks and nightly regression, including our in-house device lab.

Result. Within months we converted 200+ Appium tests to Espresso and built an iOS suite of hundreds of tests. Flakiness became rare: nearly every failure pointed to a real app bug or a feature change. At our first demo, Espresso ran so fast that everyone missed it, so we replayed the recording in slow motion.

What it taught me. Learning Protractor and TypeScript from scratch taught me not to give up on unfamiliar tools. WeightWatchers turned that into confidence. And when a framework keeps fighting you, step back and explore the other options available instead of forcing it.

Android · Open source · 2026

Totebox: an Android shop and its Espresso test suite

I built a small Android shopping app from scratch as a realistic test target, with login rules, a cart with saved items, checkout validation, a swipe-to-order slider and a deliberate crash. Then I automated it with Espresso: page objects, an IdlingResource instead of sleeps, Allure reports, and JVM unit tests for the business rules.
